Executive Summary

ExplorePune is a web-based city guide that transforms Pune's heritage sites into interactive, visual, 3D experiences — accessible to anyone with a browser, for free. It is the only Pune resource that combines deep editorial content, procedural 3D walkthroughs, and a community feedback loop in one place.

The opportunity

  • Pune attracts over 2 million domestic tourists annually (pre-pandemic baseline and growing), plus a large student and business-visitor population that lives in the city year-round.
  • Heritage tourism is underserved — existing information is scattered across blogs, Wikipedia entries, and YouTube videos, with no consistent visual preview of what a place actually looks like.
  • Digital guidebooks are a proven category — Lonely Planet, TripAdvisor, and city-specific guides all show that travelers want curated, visual, trustworthy information before they commit to a visit.
  • 3D walkthroughs are a differentiator — no competing Pune guide offers this. It is a moat, not a feature.

What ExplorePune delivers

  • 15 hand-picked heritage places — forts, temples, gardens, museums, memorials, lakes, and hilltops — each with a dedicated 3D diorama you can orbit, zoom, and fly through.
  • Curated editorial depth — history, architecture, what to see, visitor etiquette, and frequently asked questions, written in plain language with Marathi names alongside English.
  • Smart directory — filter by category, area, season, audience, and free/paid entry; switch between grid, list, and Google Maps views.
  • Community layer — visitors leave reviews, suggest missing places, and moderators approve submissions through an admin dashboard.
In one sentence: ExplorePune takes Pune's heritage out of scattered blog posts and static photos and puts it into a single, visual, interactive, free destination on the web — built so that adding more places costs time, not money and not an art pipeline.

What a visitor struggles with today

  • Scattered information — one blog covers Shaniwar Wada's history, another covers its opening hours, a third covers the light-and-sound show. Nothing brings it together.
  • No visual preview — a visitor can't tell from a photo whether a fort is a 20-minute walk or a 3-hour trek, whether the temple courtyard is intimate or vast, whether the garden is worth the entry fee.
  • Planning friction — opening hours, entry fees, best season, nearby places, and how to reach a site require jumping across multiple sources, many of which are out of date.
  • Seasonal context is invisible — Empress Garden in January during the flower show is a completely different experience from a Tuesday in May. No existing guide visualises that difference.

What locals and residents miss

  • Hidden gems stay hidden — Pataleshwar Cave Temple (an 8th-century Rashtrakuta cave under JM Road) and Khadakwasla Dam's eleven radial sluices are real architectural and engineering landmarks that most Pune residents walk past without knowing.
  • No community voice — there is no easy way to suggest a place that is missing, or to read real visitor experiences curated alongside editorial content.
  • Educational use cases go unmet — schools and colleges teaching Indian history, architecture, or tourism have no visual, web-native resource they can point students to.
ExplorePune turns this around. A visitor opens the site, filters to what interests them, and within a minute can see a place in 3D, read its story, and understand whether it is worth the trip.

What ExplorePune Is — And How It Works

Browse and filter

Land on the homepage and see category tiles for Forts & Palaces, Temples & Spiritual, Museums & Memorials, Nature & Gardens, and Lakes & Hills. Featured places are ranked by visitor rating. Scroll through curated collections like "Sunday Morning Forts" or "Places with a View."

Move to the Places page and filter by what matters: category, area of the city, who the place suits (families, solo travelers, students, photography enthusiasts), the season, and whether entry is free or paid. Switch between a photo grid, a compact list, and a Google Maps view — all from one set of filters.

15 places, 5 categories

Forts & Palaces — Shaniwar Wada, Lal Mahal, Sinhagad Fort, Aga Khan Palace

Museums & Memorials — Raja Dinkar Kelkar Museum, National War Memorial Southern Command

Temples & Spiritual — Dagdusheth Halwai Ganapati, Pataleshwar Cave, Parvati Hill, Osho Resort

Nature & Gardens — Okayama Friendship Garden, Saras Baug, Empress Garden

Lakes & Hills — Vetal Tekdi, Khadakwasla Dam

Open any place and step into it

Every place opens with a 3D scene you control directly. Drag to orbit — look around the fort walls, circle the temple, hover over the garden canopy. Scroll to zoom in and out. Tap a numbered hotspot and the camera flies smoothly to that landmark — the Delhi Darwaza at Shaniwar Wada, the black pyramid at the Osho Resort, the eternal flame at the National War Memorial.

When the camera lands, the story loads: the history, the architecture, what to look for, and the questions visitors actually ask. Each feature is shown in English with its Marathi name beside it.

Three ways to shift the mood

Time of day — switch every scene between dawn, golden hour, and dusk. The sky, the sun, the shadows, and the fog all change together. A fort at dusk reads differently from the same fort at noon, and the scene shows you that.

Season or festival mode — Empress Garden has a show-week toggle that places exhibition tents and extra bloom on the lawns, so you can see the garden during the January flower show or on a quiet Tuesday.

Camera flight — every hotspot has a defined camera anchor — a target point, a viewing direction, and a distance. Tapping a hotspot interpolates the camera to that position so the transition feels like moving through the space, not jumping between photos.

Plan the actual visit

Every place guide includes the practical layer: how to reach it (by road, approximate time from the city centre), visitor tips, opening hours where known, entry fee status, and what nearby places pair well with it. A family visiting Shaniwar Wada can see, in the same place, that Aga Khan Palace and the National War Memorial are both a short drive away — and open those places in new tabs from the same page.

The directory's map view plots every place on a Google Map centred on Pune (18.5204°N, 73.8567°E), so a visitor planning a half-day route can see the geography directly.

What's in every place guide

  • Cover — the place name, category badge, and visual identity
  • 3D experience — the interactive diorama with hotspots
  • Story — 2–3 narrative sections: the history, the character of the place
  • Features — 4–6 numbered landmarks with blurbs and full detail
  • Stats — labelled facts (acres, founding year, notable events)
  • Etiquette — visitor do's and don'ts, often with seasonal notes
  • FAQs — 4–5 questions with grounded answers
  • Practical info — how to reach, tips, nearby places

The community loop

Leave a review — visitors rate and comment on places. Reviews are stored in the shared data store. In the demo they persist across sessions when a Redis store is connected; the architecture supports real per-user persistence as the next step.

Suggest a place — a form lets anyone propose a heritage site that is missing from the guide. Submissions go into the admin queue.

Upload a photo — visitors can attach up to six photos (JPG, PNG, WEBP, GIF, under 4 MB each) to accompany a suggestion or review. Photos are stored in the cloud (Vercel Blob) when the project is configured, or locally for self-hosting.

Admin dashboard

A moderator view lets the maintainer approve or reject place suggestions and moderate reviews. This keeps the community layer curated rather than open — a deliberate choice for a heritage guide where accuracy matters.

Why this matters for growth: every community submission is a potential new place in the guide. The cost of adding a place is time, not money — so the community loop is a genuine growth engine, not a feature.

The Cost Decision: Procedural 3D vs. Imported 3D Assets

This is the single most important cost decision in the entire project — and the one that determines whether a Pune-only guide is affordable at all.

Visual comparison

The bar chart shows the 3D-specific cost line for both approaches across 15 places. The procedural approach costs roughly 6× less in direct 3D spend — and incidentally delivers zero asset weight to the visitor, faster iteration, and a testable codebase.

Why this matters for the business: the procedural approach is not just cheaper — it makes the project viable. A Pune-only guide built with imported 3D assets would cost ₹3–7.5 lakh in 3D alone, before any dev or content cost. The same guide built procedurally costs ₹30,000–1.2 lakh in 3D dev time. That difference is the difference between a project that gets built and one that stalls in planning.

Revenue Potential & Business Model

ExplorePune is free for every visitor today. That is a deliberate choice — it maximizes reach, trust, and the community loop. But a free product still has a revenue surface, and the surface grows as the audience grows.

1. Voluntary support (today)

An in-site UPI tip jar — explorepune@ybl — lets appreciative visitors contribute directly. This is not a revenue engine, but it is a signal: it tells the maintainer that people value the product enough to pay for it voluntarily.

Revenue potential: small, irregular, goodwill-based. A meaningful early signal if it trends upward.

2. Sponsored listings (near-term)

Heritage tour operators, local guides, and nearby hotels can pay for a clearly-labeled sponsored placement alongside editorial content — for example, a "Heritage Walk of Old Pune" operator listed next to Shaniwar Wada, Lal Mahal, and Khadakwasla Dam.

Revenue potential: ₹2,000–10,000 per listing per month, depending on placement and traffic. A handful of listings could cover the monthly run-rate.

3. Partnerships & affiliate (medium-term)

Affiliate links for nearby hotels, transport, and entry-ticket bookings. Co-branded content with Maharashtra Tourism or local heritage Trusts. A Marathi-language version opens a new audience segment.

Revenue potential: scales with audience. Affiliate conversion on a tourism site is typically 1–3% of visitors who book something after clicking.

Audience-to-revenue funnel (illustrative)

Illustrative funnel based on typical visitor-behaviour patterns for visual guide products. The top of the funnel — visitors who open the site — is the largest and cheapest to grow (organic search, social sharing, word of mouth). The narrowest stage — visitors who convert or sponsor — is where revenue lives, and it grows as the audience and trust grow.

What revenue needs first

  • Audience volume — revenue is a function of visitors. The free model maximises the top of the funnel.
  • Trust and quality — a sponsored listing next to sloppy content destroys trust. The editorial depth is what makes sponsored placements credible.
  • A clear labelling standard — sponsored content must be unmistakably labelled so the visitor never confuses it with editorial.

What revenue does not need

  • A paywall — putting Pune's heritage behind a paywall would shrink the audience and the community loop. Free is the right strategy.
  • Ads clutter — banner ads would degrade the visual experience that is the product's differentiator. Sponsored placements and affiliate links are cleaner.
  • A large team — the procedural architecture means the product scales with one developer's time, not headcount.

The Visitor Journey — From Discovery to Visit

A visitor moves through ExplorePune in a predictable path. Each stage has an engagement behaviour and a measurable signal. The product is designed so that each stage leads naturally to the next.

1. Land and browse — the top of the funnel

A visitor arrives from search, social, or a direct link. The homepage presents category tiles, featured places, and curated collections. Within seconds they can see what the site covers and filter to what interests them.

Signal: category tile clicks, time on homepage, filter usage. A visitor who filters is a visitor who is seriously considering a visit.

2. Open a place and step into the 3D scene — the differentiator

The visitor taps a place. The cover renders with the category badge and metadata; the 3D scene mounts client-side via a dynamic import. They drag to orbit, scroll to zoom, and — this is the key behaviour — tap a numbered hotspot. The camera flies to that landmark and the story loads.

Signal: hotspot taps, camera flight completions, time spent in the 3D scene. A visitor who taps two or more hotspots is engaged enough to consider the place seriously.

3. Read the story and plan — the decision layer

With the 3D scene open and the story visible, the visitor reads the history, the features, the etiquette, and the FAQs. They check how to reach the place, the entry fee status, and nearby places. The map view in the directory helps them see geography.

Signal: story section scrolls, FAQ expansion, "nearby places" clicks. A visitor who reads the etiquette is a visitor who is preparing to visit respectfully.

4. Visit, then return and contribute — the community loop

The visitor goes to the place. Later, they return to the site to leave a review, upload a photo, or suggest a place that was missing. The admin dashboard curates these contributions. Over time, the guide gets richer — and the next visitor has a better experience.

Signal: review submissions, photo uploads, place suggestions. A visitor who contributes is a visitor who becomes a small part of the product's growth.

Engagement design principles

  • Fast first paint — the editorial shell renders server-side. The 3D scene loads only when the visitor scrolls to it. A visitor on a slow connection still sees the story immediately.
  • No dead ends — every place links to nearby places, the directory, and the suggest-a-place form. A visitor who finishes reading a story can go somewhere meaningful next.
  • Respect the visit — etiquette and FAQs are written for a real visitor, not an SEO keyword list. That builds trust, and trust is what brings visitors back and brings them to contribute.

Why this journey converts

  • The 3D scene is the hook — it is the one thing no competitor offers, and it is what makes a visitor stay and explore.
  • The editorial depth is the reason to trust — a visitor who reads a well-written story about Shaniwar Wada is more likely to visit it than one who skimmed a photo gallery.
  • The community loop is the growth engine — every contribution makes the product better for the next visitor, at near-zero marginal cost.

Why Pune Only — And What It Means for Cost and Strategy

ExplorePune is deliberately focused on one city. That focus is not a limitation of the technology — the architecture can be extended to other cities — but a deliberate business choice about where to start and what to optimise for.

Why start with one city

  • Depth over breadth — 15 places with real editorial depth and a real 3D scene each are worth more than 100 places with a placeholder. A Pune visitor who opens the site gets a complete, trustworthy guide, not a thin directory.
  • Curation is the product — the value is not the number of places but the quality of the writing, the accuracy of the 3D, and the trustworthiness of the etiquette and FAQs. That takes time per place, and one city is a realistic scope for one author.
  • Word of mouth is local — a Pune resident who loves the guide tells other Pune residents. A guide that covers all of Maharashtra dilutes that local word-of-mouth density. A Pune-only guide can own the phrase "the Pune heritage guide."
  • Cost discipline — building one city well is affordable (see the cost slide). Building ten cities at once is not — and a half-built ten-city guide is worse than a complete Pune guide.

What Pune-only means for cost

  • Build cost is bounded — 15 places, one city, one author. The cost estimate on the previous slide is realistic for Pune only. Adding a second city would roughly double the 3D and editorial cost — and that is a decision to make after Pune is live and proven.
  • Ongoing cost is low — free-tier hosting, free-tier Redis, free-tier Maps, and a small content update budget. A Pune-only guide can run on a shoestring.
  • Revenue per visitor is higher — a Pune visitor is more likely to be interested in a sponsored Pune heritage walk than a visitor who landed on a Maharashtra-wide guide looking for a specific site. Focus improves relevance, and relevance improves conversion.
  • Expansion is a later decision — the architecture supports adding cities. The procedural pipeline can be reused. But the decision to expand should come after Pune has proven the model, not before.
The Pune-only strategy in one line: build one city so well that it becomes the definitive Pune heritage guide — and let the cost, the curation, and the word of mouth all compound in one place before expanding.

Sensitivity: What Drives the Numbers

A bootstrapped Pune-only guide like this does not need venture-scale returns to be worthwhile. But it helps to see which variables move the outcome — audience size, revenue per visitor, and the cost of adding places.

Scenario table — first-year outcomes

Visitors per year — a function of SEO, social sharing, word of mouth, and Pune tourism volume. The moderate scenario (80,000 visitors/year) is a reasonable target for a useful, free, Pune-specific guide that ranks for heritage-site searches.

Revenue sensitivity: at ₹5 revenue per 100 visitors (a conservative blended rate across sponsored listings, affiliate clicks, and voluntary tips), 80,000 visitors/year yields ~₹4,000/month in revenue — enough to cover the cloud run-rate and contribute to content refresh. At ₹20 per 100 visitors (a few sponsored listings actively converting), the same audience yields ~₹16,000/month — enough to pay a part-time content maintainer.

Break-even thinking

At the moderate audience scenario (80,000 visitors/year) and a ₹10/100-visitor revenue rate, the guide generates ~₹8,000/month — enough to cover the cloud run-rate and a small content budget. The build cost (₹85,000–2,60,000) pays back within 12–30 months at that rate — before counting any expansion value or audience growth beyond the moderate scenario.

This is not a venture-scale return profile — it is a bootstrapped,/utility-scale return profile. That is the right profile for a free, public-interest heritage guide.

Roadmap — From Pune to a Defensible Guide

Now — Pune, complete and live (months 0–3)

Ship the remaining places to their full 3D treatment. Connect a real Upstash Redis store so reviews and suggestions persist. Populate community photo uploads. Add curated route collections — for example, "Old City Morning: Shaniwar Wada, Lal Mahal, Parvati Hill" — that combine nearby places into a walkable half-day plan.

Near-term — deepen the revenue surface (months 3–6)

Onboard the first sponsored listings — heritage tour operators and local guides who serve Pune visitors directly. Add affiliate links for nearby hotels and entry-ticket bookings where available. Begin a mobile polish pass: the 3D scenes already work on touch devices; the UI polish makes the experience feel native on a phone.

Medium-term — grow the audience and the language surface (months 6–12)

Add audio guides using TTS — the data layer already has Marathi names; a voice layer turns a 3D flight between hotspots into a narrated tour. Begin a Marathi-language version of the editorial content. Reach out to schools, colleges, and heritage walk organisers who can use the dioramas as visual material — institutional use is a high-trust, high-volume audience channel.

Later — the expansion question (year 1+)

Only after Pune is the definitive Pune heritage guide: the architecture can extend to another city — Aurangabad, Nashik, Kolhapur — by copying the per-place folder pattern and writing new 3D worlds. Each new city is a deliberate decision, not a feature toggle. The procedural pipeline is reusable; the editorial depth and curation are not — and they are the point of the product.

What success looks like in year one

  • Pune visitors treat ExplorePune as the first place they check before visiting any heritage site in the city.
  • The guide has 80,000+ visitors/year — enough to cover cloud costs and a small content budget.
  • A handful of sponsored listings and affiliate links cover the monthly run-rate.
  • Community contributions (reviews, photos, suggestions) are a steady trickle — the guide gets richer without the author writing every word.
  • Schools and heritage walk operators use the dioramas as visual material — a new audience segment that does not compete with the tourist audience.

What failure would look like — and how to avoid it

  • Thin content — 15 places with weak writing and placeholder 3D. Avoidable: the editorial depth and procedural quality are the product; cut the scope to fewer places if needed, but never ship thin.
  • No audience — a great guide no one finds. Avoidable: SEO for heritage-site queries, social sharing of the 3D scenes (they are visually distinctive), and word of mouth from Pune residents.
  • Revenue before trust — monetising before the guide earns trust. Avoidable: keep the free model and the clear labelling standard; let revenue grow as audience and trust grow.
